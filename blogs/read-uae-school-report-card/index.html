How to Read a UAE School Report Card Like an Education Counsellor

Report card comments follow patterns most parents miss. A UAE education counsellor decodes the phrases teachers use, and what they actually mean.

Written by: Nimra Shahzada — Education Counsellor at Ustaad UAE
Reviewed by: Nida Iqbal — MPhil in Education Leadership and Management

Report card season creates a specific silence in a UAE household. The envelope opens. The grades get read first, then the comments. Most parents give the comments a scan, feel reassured or worried, and move on.

The comments were not written to be scanned. Teachers in KHDA and ADEK-registered schools spend hours on report writing at the end of every term. Every phrase is chosen. What looks like a compliment sometimes carries a small warning. What looks like a warning is sometimes routine language. In my counselling work with UAE families, I often go through reports line by line with parents who thought everything was fine. Nine times out of ten, the same comments read differently once you know the pattern.

01

Why Report Cards Are Written More Carefully Than Parents Realise

Teachers write comments knowing three audiences will read them:

  • The parent: needs enough clarity to respond, but not language that alarms.
  • The head of year: needs enough specificity to track progress across terms.
  • The student: a Year 10 will read the comment and take it personally.

That triple audience shapes the language. A comment cannot be too direct, because a Year 10 student will read it and take it to heart. It cannot be too vague, because the head of year needs to track progress. It cannot be alarmist, because it goes home to a family the school wants to keep supporting.

The result is a diplomatic style that carries information underneath the surface. Every experienced UAE teacher has a vocabulary for signalling 'this student needs help' without writing those words in a document that stays on the school record. This is not evasive. It is professional. But it means parents have to read the report differently from how they read a text message.

02

The Three Layers of a School Report

Every comment on a UAE school report carries three layers. Read all three together, or the surface layer alone will mislead.

LAYER 1 · SURFACE

The sentence the parent reads at first glance.

LAYER 2 · SECOND

What the teacher observed in class over the term.

LAYER 3 · THIRD

What the teacher recommends, translated into report-card-safe language.

A comment like 'A pleasant member of the class who contributes when prompted' looks positive. The surface says the student is well behaved. The second layer says the student rarely volunteers answers. The third layer, the recommendation, is that the student is coasting and needs pushing. Once parents can see all three layers, most report cards start telling a fuller story.

03

Decoding the Most Common Phrases

Each phrase below has a surface reading (what parents hear) and a working meaning (what the teacher intended). The working meaning is the one that matters.

"Making steady progress"

Decoded 🔍
Surface Reading (What Parents Hear)

The student is doing well.

Working Meaning (What Teacher Intended)
The student is meeting expectations and no more. No acceleration, no standout, no concern. Fine at Year 7-8. In a Year 10 or 11 report before IGCSE mocks, this can be a soft warning that the student is not yet at the level required for a strong final grade.

"Would benefit from..."

Decoded 🔍
Surface Reading (What Parents Hear)

A gentle suggestion the teacher is making.

Working Meaning (What Teacher Intended)
The most polite way a UAE teacher tells you something is missing. 'Would benefit from consistent homework' signals that homework is being missed. Read every 'would benefit from' as 'currently is not, and needs to'.

"Encouraged to..."

Decoded 🔍
Surface Reading (What Parents Hear)

The teacher is nudging the student.

Working Meaning (What Teacher Intended)
Similar to 'would benefit from', slightly softer. A coaching prompt the teacher wants recorded without formally flagging a concern. 'Encouraged to review notes daily' usually means the student is only revising at the last minute.

"Has shown improvement in..."

Decoded 🔍
Surface Reading (What Parents Hear)

Something has got better.

Working Meaning (What Teacher Intended)
The reverse tell. When a teacher singles out improvement in one specific area, it often means that area was the problem. If the comment says 'improvement in showing full working', earlier reports likely flagged missing working.

"Works well when focused"

Decoded 🔍
Surface Reading (What Parents Hear)

The student can work well.

Working Meaning (What Teacher Intended)
Watch the conditional. 'When focused'. 'When engaged'. The teacher is saying the student has ability but is not consistently applying it. Very common on Year 9-10 capable students, and almost always precedes a Year 11 dip if nothing changes.

"A pleasure to teach"

Decoded 🔍
Surface Reading (What Parents Hear)

The teacher likes the student.

Working Meaning (What Teacher Intended)
Genuinely positive. But when it appears alone with no academic observation, the teacher may be avoiding writing anything critical. Behaviour comments are safe territory. When they fill the whole box, look at the grade instead.

"Silence in the comment box"

Decoded 🔍
Surface Reading (What Parents Hear)

The teacher was busy.

Working Meaning (What Teacher Intended)
If a teacher used to write two or three lines and this term writes only 'good work this term', that shift is worth reading. The teacher may have a specific concern they chose not to record, preferring to open a conversation instead. Ask for that conversation.

"The subject-specific tells"

Decoded 🔍
Surface Reading (What Parents Hear)

Standard subject-specific phrasing.

Working Meaning (What Teacher Intended)
In biology: watch for 'would benefit from more precise terminology' or 'encouraged to link structure to function'. In chemistry: any comment on the mole, stoichiometry, or practical work is worth taking seriously. See why chemistry fades from memory and early signs your child needs help in chemistry for the pattern in more depth.
04

Effort Grades and Attainment Grades: The Mismatch That Matters

Most UAE reports carry two grades per subject: an effort grade (sometimes called approach or attitude) and an attainment grade. Parents read them together. The gap between the two carries the most information, and it maps neatly onto four quadrants.

LOW ATTAINMENT
HIGH ATTAINMENT
HIGH EFFORT / LOW ATTAINMENT

Approach is wrong

Student is trying but the study method is off. A good problem to have; effort is harder to build. Fix is in study method or exam technique, not motivation.

HIGH EFFORT / HIGH ATTAINMENT

Healthy state

Comments here will usually be detailed and specific. A suddenly generic comment feels different.

LOW EFFORT / LOW ATTAINMENT

Fastest response needed

Both metrics dropping in the same term is rarely accidental. Book a meeting.

LOW EFFORT / HIGH ATTAINMENT

Coasting on ability

Looks fine until Year 11 or 12 content jumps. Comments usually include 'has capacity for more'.

The follow-up piece why students understand physics but lose marks covers the classic high effort, low attainment pattern.

05

When the Report Card and Your Child's Words Do Not Match

This is the moment parents notice first: the report shows a drop, and the child says everything is fine. Or the report is glowing, and the child seems worried.

In my experience, the child is usually giving you information the report cannot capture. A Year 12 student who says 'chemistry feels heavier this year' but has an A- on the report is telling you the effort behind that A- has doubled. A Year 10 student who says 'maths is fine' but has a lower grade than last term is often protecting the parent from their own frustration, or protecting themselves from a difficult conversation.

When there is a mismatch, take the child's version seriously. Reports show what has already happened, not what is happening now. What a student feels this week usually shows up on the next report, not the current one. If the child mentions anything about stress, sleep, or exam nerves, the blog what UAE parents miss about exam panic is worth reading before deciding on next steps.

06

What to Do With the Report Card in the Next 48 Hours

The first 48 hours after a report arrives shape whether the term ahead improves or repeats the same pattern.

1
HOUR 1 · FIRST READRead Twice

Read the comments twice before saying anything. The first read is emotional. The second read is where the pattern shows.

2
HOUR 2 · CONVERSATIONAsk Specifics

Do not lead with the grade. Ask about a specific comment. 'Your maths teacher wrote that you would benefit from checking your working. What do you think she meant?' This gets a real answer. 'Why did you get a B?' does not.

3
SAME DAY · COMPARISONTrack Trajectory

Compare this term's comments to last term's for the same subject. One report tells you where the student is. Two consecutive reports tell you where they are moving.

4
DAY 2 · ACTION PLANCalm Decisions

Wait until day two before deciding whether extra support is needed. Reports read differently after a night's sleep. Panic-booking tutors on the same evening rarely leads to the right match.

07

When to Ask the School for a Meeting

A meeting is worth requesting in three situations:

  1. 1When a comment contains language you do not fully understand and you want the teacher to translate. Schools appreciate parents who ask rather than assume.
  2. 2When the effort grade and the attainment grade have moved in opposite directions. One of them is telling a story the report format has flattened.
  3. 3When a subject-specific comment references a topic your child cannot explain at home.

Meetings work best when they are booked around a specific question. 'I want to understand what changed in chemistry between term one and term two' is answerable. 'I am worried about the report' is harder to respond to.
