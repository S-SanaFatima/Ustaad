Exams Are Back. What Changes for Students Who Have Never Sat One

UAE exams are returning after a year of portfolio grading. Here is what changes for students who have never sat a real paper, and how parents can help.

Nimra Shahzada

Somewhere in Dubai sits a Year 11 student with a full set of IGCSE grades on paper, a strong report card, and a place in sixth form, who has never once sat in a hall and answered against a clock.

That is not a criticism. Last summer's main series in the UAE was cancelled, and grades came through a portfolio of evidence instead. Results were good. Schools were relieved. Families moved on.

But the next series is expected to run in full. The students preparing for it are the first group in years to walk into an exam hall without a single previous paper behind them. No mocks that counted. No timed papers under real conditions. No memory of what the third hour of a long paper feels like.

Parents have noticed something is different. Most cannot quite say what. This guide names it plainly, and shows what you can do.

01

What actually happened, in plain terms

In 2026, the three main boards used by British-curriculum schools in the UAE (Cambridge International, Pearson Edexcel and OxfordAQA) cancelled the summer series here. The reason was regional security, not the students or the schools.

Instead of papers, students were graded through a portfolio of evidence. Schools gathered mocks, coursework, classwork and internal assessments. Board examiners marked and moderated that evidence. The IB applied a similar contingency route for Diploma students.

It worked. Grades were issued on time and, across the UAE, results were strong. What it also did, quietly, is remove the one experience every previous cohort had taken for granted, at exactly the age when that experience is usually learned.

02

Portfolio grading measured something real. It just was not this.

A portfolio grade is not a fake grade. It reflects work a student genuinely did over months, marked by real examiners. If your child earned strong grades this way, they earned them.

But a portfolio measures how well a student can learn and produce when they have time, notes, a teacher nearby, and the chance to revise a draft. An exam measures something different: whether they can produce what they know, from memory, in a fixed format, in a fixed window, alone.

Those are not the same skill. Having one does not give you the other. That is the gap parents are sensing. Their child is capable, has proof of being capable, and has never been asked to show it the way the next paper will demand.

Parent takeaway:Your child's grades are real. What they have not yet built is the skill of producing those grades under exam conditions. That needs separate practice.

03

The five things an exam asks for that coursework never did

None of these are about content. Your child may know the syllabus well. These are about what the hall itself demands.

1. Producing Knowledge Cold

Cognitive Retrieval

In coursework, knowledge is retrieved with support. Notes are nearby, teachers can clarify prompts, and students can pause. In an exam, retrieval is completely unassisted. A student who spent the previous year rereading may recognise every topic and still freeze when required to retrieve formulas, definitions, or mechanisms from memory.

Training drill: 10-minute closed-book brain dumps of key concepts before attempting past paper questions.

2. Working Against the Clock

Time Allocation

A two-hour paper is not two hours of thinking. It is 120 minutes minus reading time, arithmetic checking, and recalculating when an answer looks wrong. Students who have never sat against a strict clock either rush early questions carelessly or linger on tough 3-mark problems and leave final pages untouched.

Training drill: Strict 1 mark = 1 minute pacing discipline with a physical countdown clock visible.

3. Sustained Cognitive Stamina

Focus Span

Concentrating intensely for 90 to 120 minutes without breaks or phone interruptions is physically and mentally demanding in a way school lessons are not. A student can understand the material completely and still fade in the final 30 minutes simply because their mental stamina has never been stretched to paper length.

Training drill: Incremental stamina ladder: 45 min unbroken focus, progressing +15 mins each week up to 105 mins.

4. Decoding Command Words Under Time

Exam Technique

Command words carry specific mark structures. "Describe" and "explain" award marks differently. "Evaluate" requires a supported conclusion. In coursework portfolios, teachers frequently prompted students to elaborate. In an external hall, markers award nothing for implied reasoning that isn't explicitly stated on the lines.

Training drill: Underline the command word and number of marks before writing any multi-mark answer.

5. Managing Adrenaline Without a Safety Net

Composure

Previous cohorts walked into finals knowing what a real paper felt like; they had survived the nerves before. For this cohort, their very first live paper may determine their final grades. Experiencing unfamiliar physical tension without previous conditioning can cause temporary cognitive blanking.

Training drill: Rehearse a reset routine: when stuck, take two deep breaths, circle the question, and move immediately to the next one.
Parent takeaway:The gap is not what your child knows. It is retrieval, timing, stamina, reading the question, and nerves. All five are trainable. None train themselves.
Case Study: Year 11, Dubai, Cambridge IGCSE Mathematics 0580

A Year 11 student in Dubai Hills finished Year 10 with a portfolio grade that mapped to a strong 7 in Cambridge 0580. Homework was accurate. Class tests with notes nearby looked solid. In her first full timed Paper 2 this term, under invigilation and with no calculator switch-outs mid-way, she left three multi-mark questions blank and scored a low 5.

The content was not missing. Cold retrieval and clock management were. After six weeks of one timed section a week, marked against the 0580 scheme with lost marks written down, her next mock moved to a secure 6 with time left to check. 04

Year 11 and Year 13 face this differently

Year 11 students sat no IGCSE or GCSE papers last year. Many also had reduced end-of-year tests in Year 10. So their first real external paper will be the one that decides their grades. The good news is they have the year ahead and schools will build mocks in. The risk is treating those mocks as low-stakes practice rather than the rehearsal they are.

Year 13 students are in a stranger position. Their A-Level grades last year came from portfolios, and those grades now feed predicted grades for university. They then face full papers with less examination experience than any A-Level cohort in living memory. For competitive courses, that matters: the offer is conditional on results from a format the student has never experienced.

Neither group is doomed. Both need to start the term already knowing the missing skill is exam craft, not content.

Case Study: Year 13, Abu Dhabi, Pearson Edexcel A-Level Chemistry 9CH0

A Year 13 student on Reem Island held a portfolio A in Edexcel 9CH0 and a predicted A for university applications. In his first school mock this autumn (Paper 1, full timing, no notes), organic mechanisms he could explain in tutorials collapsed under time. He finished twenty minutes early with thin answers on the last two questions and dropped a full grade.

What changed the next month was not more note-making. It was weekly timed 9CH0 sections, stamina blocks that grew from forty to ninety minutes, and treating every mock as invigilated. His second mock recovered to the predicted band. 05

What parents can actually do

You do not need to become an examiner. Four things make a real difference.

Get timed practice into the term early

Most families start timed past papers a few weeks before exam season. This year that is too late. From early in the term, one timed paper section a week, marked honestly against the mark scheme, does more than any amount of extra revision. The point is not the score. The point is working to a clock and seeing exactly where marks went.

If your child has not started past papers yet, why past papers are the final step, not the first explains how to sequence them properly. For families building a fuller plan, see also GCSE revision tips for UAE parents and our exam preparation overview.

Treat every mock as the real thing

Schools are running mocks more formally this year, partly because last year mock results were submitted as evidence. Encourage your child to treat them that way too: full timed conditions, no notes, the whole paper in one go. A mock taken seriously is the closest rehearsal your child will get.

Build stamina like a physical skill

Because that is what it is. Start with forty-minute focused sessions and extend by ten minutes each week until your child can hold concentration for a full paper length. This is separate from revision. It is training the ability to sit, focus and produce without a break.

Watch for avoidance, not just anxiety

A child who is scared of exams sometimes looks anxious. More often, at this age, they look uninterested. Homework gets put off. Past papers get "forgotten". Revision becomes rereading, because rereading feels safe and testing does not.

That avoidance is the signal. If you see it, the answer is not more pressure. It is making the next step small enough to be obviously doable. Not "revise chemistry." "Do these six questions with a timer on."

Parent takeaway:Your job is the routine and the conditions, not the teaching. Timed practice early, mocks taken seriously, stamina built gradually, and an eye on avoidance. That is the whole list.

07

Bringing it together

The students sitting papers this year are not less capable than the ones before them. Many have shown more resilience, finishing courses through a disrupted year and earning grades through sustained work rather than one afternoon.

What they lack is narrow and specific: the craft of the exam hall. Retrieval. Timing. Stamina. Reading the question. Steady nerves. Previous cohorts absorbed those almost by accident. This cohort will have to learn them on purpose.

That is not bad news. Skills learned on purpose tend to be learned better. But it does mean the year cannot be treated as normal, and the mocks cannot be treated as a formality.

If your child is in Year 11 or Year 13, this is the year to make exam practice a habit, not a last-minute scramble. Start early, keep it steady, and let the paper, not the panic, tell you where the work needs to go.

See where your child stands before the mocks do

A short diagnostic session does one thing: it puts your child in front of a timed section of their own board's paper, marks it honestly, and tells you which of the five skills above needs work. No teaching on the first day. Just a clear picture of where the gap is.
